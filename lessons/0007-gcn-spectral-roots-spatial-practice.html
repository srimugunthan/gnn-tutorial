Lesson 7

GCN: Spectral Roots, Spatial Practice

Lessons 4–6 gave you the generic message-passing recipe — gather messages from a node's neighborhood, aggregate them with a permutation-invariant function, update the node's representation — and showed what stacking that recipe does to a node's receptive field. Now we specialise. The Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) of Kipf & Welling (2017) was the architecture that made this recipe concrete and fast enough to matter, and it did so by way of a surprising detour through spectral graph theory.

The limitation it solves: convolution needs a "graph Fourier transform"

Before GCN, the natural way to define "convolution on a graph" was to borrow directly from signal processing. A CNN's convolution is a multiplication in the frequency domain, made cheap by the Fast Fourier Transform. Graphs have an analogous frequency basis: the eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian L = D − A. A "spectral graph convolution" filters a signal x by transforming it into this eigenbasis, multiplying by a learned filter gθ, and transforming back.

This works mathematically, but it's impractical:

  • Eigendecomposition is O(|V|3) — infeasible for graphs with more than a few thousand nodes.
  • The learned filter is global, not localized to a neighborhood — every node's filter depends on the entire spectrum, which also means the number of filter parameters scales with graph size and can't transfer to a differently-sized graph.

Kipf & Welling's contribution was to approximate this spectral filter so aggressively that both problems vanish, leaving behind a rule that looks purely spatial — a weighted average over a node's immediate neighbors, exactly the shape of the message-passing framework from Lesson 4.

From spectral filter to spatial rule

The derivation goes through two approximation steps:

  1. Chebyshev truncation. Approximate the spectral filter gθ with a truncated Chebyshev polynomial expansion of the Laplacian, up to order K. This avoids the eigendecomposition entirely — polynomials of L can be applied directly — and a K-th order polynomial is exactly K-hop localized, since (LK)ij is nonzero only when i and j are within K hops.
  2. First-order truncation (K=1), linearized. GCN takes this to the extreme: K=1, and even the single remaining coefficient is fixed rather than learned. What's left is a filter that only looks at direct neighbors per layer — depth, not filter order, is what extends the reach, exactly as Lesson 5 described for the receptive field.

After this collapse, and after a further trick to keep the eigenvalues of the propagation operator numerically well-behaved (the renormalization trick), the entire derivation reduces to one equation applied at every layer:

The GCN propagation rule:
H(l+1) = σ( D̃−1/2 Ã D̃−1/2 H(l) W(l) )

where Ã = A + IN (adjacency matrix with self-loops added), D̃ is the degree matrix of Ã (so D̃ii = Σj Ãij), H(l) is the matrix of node representations at layer l (H(0) = X), W(l) is a learned weight matrix shared across all nodes, and σ is a nonlinearity like ReLU.

Self-loops (Ã = A + I) are what let a node's own features survive into the aggregate — recall from Lesson 2 that this was foreshadowed as "we'll see exactly why this matters when we derive the GCN update rule." Without them, a node's representation at each layer would depend only on its neighbors, never on itself.

Reading the rule as message passing

Mapped onto the gather → aggregate → update vocabulary from Lesson 4:

  • Message: each neighbor j sends its current representation hj, linearly transformed by the shared W.
  • Aggregate: a weighted sum over neighbors (including the node itself, via the self-loop), where the weight between i and j is D̃ii−1/2 Ãij D̃jj−1/2.
  • Update: apply the nonlinearity σ to the aggregated result.

The key design choice to notice: the aggregation weights are fixed by graph structure (they depend only on degrees), not learned or content-dependent. Every neighbor's contribution is determined the moment you know the graph — GCN doesn't ask "how relevant is this neighbor's content?" the way an attention-based architecture will in Lesson 9. It only asks "how many neighbors do you and I each have?"

Analogy: D̃−1/2 Ã D̃−1/2 is a symmetric normalization — it downweights a message twice: once for the sender's degree, once for the receiver's degree. A high-degree "hub" neighbor gets less weight per edge (its influence is spread across many neighbors), and a node with many neighbors discounts each incoming message more (it has more messages to average over). It's the graph analogue of dividing by fan-in and fan-out.

Worked example: one GCN update, by hand

Reuse the four-node graph from Lesson 2 (A–B, A–C, B–C, C–D), and compute the aggregation weights feeding into node A after adding self-loops:

Node A's neighbors-with-self-loop are {A, B, C}, with degrees (post self-loop) D̃AA=3, D̃BB=3, D̃CC=4. The normalized weight for each edge is 1/√(D̃iiD̃jj):

weight(A←A) = 1/√(3×3) = 0.333   (self-loop term)
weight(A←B) = 1/√(3×3) = 0.333
weight(A←C) = 1/√(3×4) = 0.289

h_A' = σ( W·(0.333·h_A + 0.333·h_B + 0.289·h_C) )

Notice node D never enters A's update directly — it's two hops away. That's the receptive-field argument from Lesson 5 again: one GCN layer only reaches 1-hop neighbors, so D's influence on A requires stacking a second layer.

Transductive by default

The propagation rule as written bakes the graph's degree matrix D̃ and adjacency Ã directly into every forward pass. That has a real consequence: the original GCN is a transductive method. It's trained and evaluated on one fixed graph — typically full-batch, using the entire normalized adjacency matrix at once. If a new node arrives after training, there's no defined procedure for folding it in without recomputing the normalization and, in the full-batch formulation, rerunning the model over the whole graph again.

Key idea: "Transductive" doesn't mean GCN can't generalize at all — it generalizes across unlabeled nodes already present in the training graph (that's the "semi-supervised" in the paper's title). What it doesn't natively support is generalizing to nodes that didn't exist at training time. That distinction becomes central in Lesson 8, and matters directly for domains like fraud detection where new accounts and transactions appear continuously.

Without normalization, a node with many neighbors would sum up many unweighted messages, causing its representation's magnitude to grow with degree. Dividing by √(degree) on both sides keeps the aggregated scale comparable across nodes regardless of how many neighbors they have.
