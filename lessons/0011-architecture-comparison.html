Lesson 11

Architecture Comparison

Four lessons, four architectures, each solving a specific limitation of the one before it: GCN made spectral convolution spatially tractable but stayed tied to one fixed graph; GraphSAGE traded fixed structural weighting for sampling and learned functions that generalize to unseen nodes; GAT traded fixed weighting for learned, content-dependent attention; GIN traded expressive weighting for guaranteed injectivity, reaching the theoretical ceiling of message-passing expressiveness. None of these is strictly "better" — each optimizes for a different constraint. This lesson builds the decision framework for picking one.

The framework isn't one axis

It's tempting to rank the four architectures on a single "power" scale. Resist that — the real decision runs along at least three largely independent axes:

  • Generalization: transductive (fixed graph, e.g. GCN as originally proposed) vs. inductive (generalizes to unseen nodes/graphs, e.g. GraphSAGE, GIN, and GAT when trained that way).
  • Weighting mechanism: structure-fixed (GCN's degree normalization), sampled/uniform (GraphSAGE's mean/pool over a sample), or learned and content-dependent (GAT's attention).
  • Expressiveness: whether the aggregation is injective enough to reach WL-test-level power (GIN), or provably weaker (GCN, GraphSAGE with mean/max, GAT in the worst case — see Lesson 10).

The important, slightly counterintuitive point from Lesson 10: expressiveness and content-adaptive weighting are different axes. GAT's attention makes weighting smarter, but an attention-weighted sum still isn't proven injective — it doesn't automatically buy WL-level power. GIN reaches the top of the expressiveness axis with a comparatively simple, uniform sum, not with attention. Don't assume "more sophisticated weighting" implies "more expressive."

A decision flowchart

As a starting heuristic — not a strict rule, since real projects often benchmark more than one — the two questions that matter most are whether you need to generalize beyond the training graph, and what the task actually demands once you're in that branch:

Applying it to real scenarios

  • Node classification on a single citation network (e.g. Cora, Citeseer), full graph known at training time: No new nodes expected, neighbors roughly interchangeable → GCN is the efficient default.
  • Recommender or social graph where you want to explain "why was this neighbor influential?": Fixed or slowly-changing graph, but neighbor relevance varies sharply → GAT, whose attention weights double as an interpretability signal.
  • Fraud/AML transaction graph where new accounts and transactions appear continuously: New nodes constantly, and you can't afford to retrain before scoring them → GraphSAGE (or GAT trained inductively) — this is precisely the "future AML application" framing from the mission.
  • Molecular property prediction where structural isomers must be told apart: Graph-level task, every molecule is a separate example (inherently inductive), and fine structural distinctions matter → GIN, with its WL-level expressiveness.
Key idea: The four architectures aren't mutually exclusive design points — they're four corners of the same design space (weighting mechanism × generalization × expressiveness). Modern practice frequently mixes ideas: a GraphSAGE-style sampled, inductive pipeline using a GIN-style sum aggregator, or an attention mechanism restricted to sampled neighbors. Knowing why each original architecture made its choice is what lets you recombine them sensibly.

Check your understanding

1. A GNN must score fraud-detection accounts that didn't exist when the model was trained. Which property should the architecture have?

  • Inductive generalization to previously unseen nodes
  • Transductive generalization to previously unseen nodes
  • Inductive memorization of previously seen nodes
  • Transductive memorization of previously seen nodes
Scoring nodes absent at training time requires inductive generalization — parameters (aggregators, attention, MLPs) that are functions of local structure and features, not tied to one fixed graph's identity, the way GraphSAGE, GIN, and inductively-trained GAT are.

2. A molecule-property task needs to distinguish two structurally different isomers with identical degree sequences. Which architecture is most likely to succeed, given Lesson 10's expressiveness results?

  • GIN, because sum aggregation is injective
  • GCN, because mean aggregation is injective
  • GAT, because attention aggregation is injective
  • GraphSAGE, because pooling aggregation is injective
GIN's sum-based aggregation, combined with an MLP, is the only one of the four proven capable of injectively encoding neighbor multisets and thus reaching WL-test-level expressiveness — exactly what's needed to tell apart structurally distinct graphs that simpler aggregators would conflate.

3. Why doesn't GAT's attention mechanism guarantee WL-level expressiveness, even though it learns content-dependent weights?

  • Attention-weighted sum is not proven to be injective
  • Attention-weighted sum is not proven to be symmetric
  • Attention-weighted sum is not able to be computed
  • Attention-weighted sum is not able to be normalized
Expressiveness and content-adaptive weighting are separate axes. Learning better weights doesn't by itself guarantee that different neighbor multisets always map to different aggregated outputs — GIN's guarantee comes from injectivity of sum-plus-MLP, not from how the weights are chosen.
